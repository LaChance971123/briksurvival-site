Bug out: evacuation destinations, triggers and routes

Build your plan ↓

A useful bug-out plan names when to leave, where to go, how to travel and what to do if the first option fails. The destination matters more than an overloaded bag.

Build your plan.

  1. 01

    Choose departure triggers

    Write hazard-specific triggers: evacuation instructions, unsafe building conditions, approaching wildfire, flood access loss or inability to sustain essential care. Leave before routes become unsafe where possible.

  2. 02

    Confirm destinations

    Arrange a nearby and an outside-area destination. Check acceptance, accessibility, pets, parking and essential care. An unconfirmed shelter or public campground is not a guaranteed destination.

  3. 03

    Plan transport and alternatives

    Map at least two practical routes where available. Consider no-car travel, fuel, road closures, bridges, water hazards and travel time with dependents.

  4. 04

    Make a short grab list

    Prioritize medicines, identification, contacts, mobility aids, keys, phone power, water and weather protection. Assign bags and dependent care; never delay an urgent departure to complete packing.

Build an evacuation plan with a real destination

“Bug out” is planned relocation from an unsafe place. Define it in ordinary terms: who leaves, under what conditions, by what transport, to which suitable destination. A vague plan to head into the countryside creates new problems with shelter, water, access, and permission. Choose locations that can actually support the household.

Keep immediate escape distinct from planned evacuation. If the building is burning or another immediate danger is present, leave without finishing a bag or route worksheet. For a developing hazard, the planning chain helps the household move before choices become limited.

Reference: American Red Cross · Make a plan · USFA · Home fire escape plans

Choose triggers you can recognize

Write conditions rather than dramatic predictions. Examples include an applicable evacuation instruction, an unsafe dwelling, or the inability to continue essential care. Some care or transport needs require an earlier departure than a general deadline. Use individual provider and equipment guidance instead of inventing a generic timing rule.

For an outdoor chemical or radiation emergency, travel can increase exposure. A relocation plan should include the possibility of sheltering under the specific instruction, rather than treating departure as always correct. Keep the hazard-specific guidance available with the trigger sheet.

Develop two destinations and check their assumptions

Choose a primary and an alternate location, considering the hazards likely to affect both. Two addresses on the same exposed street may share the same failure. Options can include a consenting trusted contact, suitable accommodation, or an operating designated shelter, but acceptance and services need checking.

  • Record the address, contact, acceptance arrangement, and how to reach it without an internet search.
  • Check access, essential equipment support, medicine storage, sleeping, water, sanitation, and any specific dietary needs.
  • Confirm pet rules and a suitable transport arrangement for the animals actually owned.
  • Identify what each location expects the household to bring.
  • Record when the information was checked and the alternative if the destination is unavailable.

Example: family offers a spare room, but the only toilet is upstairs and a household member cannot use stairs. The address is willing but not suitable without a workable accommodation. Resolve that detail before calling it the primary destination. Do not turn a friendly offer into a promise of medical or specialist support.

Reference: American Red Cross · Make a plan

Connect routes, vehicles, and carrying capacity

Choose more than one route where practical, but regard them as options to check against current conditions. A paper map or saved route note supports orientation; it does not certify that a road is open. Follow applicable closures and hazard-specific instructions and do not drive through flooded or actively dangerous routes.

Walking is not simply the same plan without a car. Reassess distance, conditions, mobility, water, carrying capacity, and route safety. Arrange support early where possible. Do not use a prolonged survival march as the default answer to an unavailable vehicle.

Prepare roles, bags, and change messages

Name who supports each dependent person, who handles pets, who manages transport, and who communicates. Include an alternate for an adult who may be away. Keep individual bags and a short list of daily-use care items ready, and test that the helpers can carry what they are assigned.

  1. For a planned departure, confirm people and urgent care requirements first.
  2. Check the destination and current route.
  3. Collect manageable bags and daily-use items if time permits.
  4. Tell the outside contact the destination, people present, and next check-in when safe.
  5. At arrival, count everyone and settle essential care.
  6. If the plan changes, send one concise updated destination message instead of relying on several different assumptions.

Protect privacy when sharing information. Trusted contacts may need the route and care requirements; a public social-media audience generally does not need the household’s exact departure, documents, or empty-home details. Communication should support safety and reunion, not create another task that delays leaving.

Keep the immediate-escape rule visible on the role sheet: no optional item is worth remaining in or re-entering danger. A beautifully packed bag should reduce departure effort, not become a reason to postpone it.

Reference: American Red Cross · Make a plan

Rehearse one failed assumption at a time

Start with a tabletop walk-through, then safe practical checks such as loading the proposed equipment or walking the normal building exit route. Do not practise a hazardous journey, shut down essential medical equipment, or enter restricted areas. Use discussion to introduce a road closure, unavailable driver, or closed destination.

  • Ask another adult to explain the trigger and find the destination information.
  • Check how children, pets, and equipment fit into the departure sequence.
  • Time ordinary retrieval only to identify a missing item or awkward arrangement, not to encourage unsafe rushing.
  • Write what changes if the primary route or location fails.
  • Review when household needs, vehicles, destinations, or local arrangements change.

After a real move, record what was actually useful and what caused delay. Replenish the bag, update the acceptance arrangements, and revise unrealistic alternatives. Plan the return separately using current access and building-safety information. The goal is a usable relocation system that can change with conditions, not an unalterable route drawn on a map.

FieldExample entry
TriggerA hazard-specific evacuation instruction applies, or essential care cannot be sustained at home.
Destination AA previously contacted relative; address, acceptance and access requirements recorded privately.
Destination BA separately checked alternative outside the same likely disruption.
TransportNamed driver or agreed pickup arrangement, capacity and fallback if unavailable.
RolesOne adult checks people and care equipment; another handles transport and the accessible bags.
MessageDeparture time, destination and next check-in sent privately to the agreed contact.

This is a template, not an assessment of a live hazard. Replace vague fields such as “leave when bad” with conditions the household can recognize. Give every role a backup and keep the card where it can be found during normal household activity. An immediate fire escape takes priority over collecting the card or completing these roles.

A destination record should include the assumptions on which it depends: permission to stay, opening/access arrangements, safe water, sanitation, pets and care. Record when it was checked. Treat a long distance and a rural setting as travel details, not proof of suitability. Review any alternative for shared dependencies, such as the same bridge or host who may also need to evacuate.

Reference: Red Cross · Make a plan

Test the decision before rehearsing the departure

Time the practical preparation separately from the travel estimate. Record how long it takes to locate daily-use items, assist dependents and load the actual transport. Do not treat a fast empty-house rehearsal as representative of the whole household. Keep the route review tied to current hazard information; several saved map lines may all share the same closure.

After the exercise, change one weak assumption rather than buying a larger bag. A missing pet acceptance check needs a destination correction. An overloaded helper needs a role or transport correction. A phone-only plan needs accessible written information. Repeat the relevant part once the correction is made, and leave the complete plan usable for another adult.

PLAN AROUND WHAT YOU HAVE

If help is unavailable.

Work from confirmed reachable options and supplies already accessible. Help may be delayed, so keep your own contacts, route notes and essential care information available without internet.

People & practical needs.

Assign an adult to each dependent and identify who can drive or arrange accessible transport. Check shelter rules for pets, service animals and support equipment before an event.

Keep essentials within reach

The trigger and role sheet, two checked destinations, current route information, transport contacts, care essentials, manageable bags and the daily-use grab list. Use an immediate-escape plan separately.

Next steps.

Time a calm departure rehearsal with the actual people, bags and transport. Record the slowest step and fix it.

Common questions.

Is a distant rural location a sufficient destination?

No. Verify access, acceptance, shelter, safe water, sanitation and essential care. An unfamiliar location can introduce needs the household is not equipped to meet.

How many routes should I have?

Develop realistic alternatives where possible and check current conditions. Several lines on a map are not helpful if they share the same closure or cannot be used by the household.

Watch for.

No universal bug-out destination or timetable suits every disaster. Avoid flooded roads and do not enter an active danger area for supplies.
