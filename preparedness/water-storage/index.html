Emergency water storage

QUICK ANSWER · START HERE

Store at least one gallon of water per person per day for three days; aim for two weeks if practical. Allow more for heat, illness, pregnancy and pets. Use commercially bottled water or clean food-grade containers, and keep it cool and away from chemicals.

Work out the household quantity

Start with a written count of people and days. CDC’s baseline is at least one U.S. gallon per person per day for three days, with a two-week supply if possible. Extra needs can include pets, hot weather, pregnancy, illness, and particular care requirements. A U.S. gallon is about 3.8 litres; do not confuse it with an imperial gallon.

Add those needs separately and identify which uses require drinking-quality water. Record the actual amount stored, not the capacity of empty containers.

Build in stages if space or money is limited

Start with a usable three-day reserve and improve it gradually. A small supply you can access, label, and maintain is more useful than an ambitious target that remains empty containers.

Choose containers you can actually handle

Unopened commercially bottled water is a straightforward emergency option. For refilled supplies, use suitable food-grade water containers, with tight lids and a design you can clean and dispense from. Never reuse a container that held toxic chemicals. Check the manufacturer if you are unsure whether a container is suitable for drinking water.

Match storage format to the household
FormatPractical advantageCheck before choosing

For planning, a five-gallon water load weighs about 42 pounds before the container. That may be too heavy for a child, an older adult, or someone with limited mobility. Choose several smaller containers if necessary. Test the empty container’s placement and dispensing position before filling it; do not plan to lift a heavy container overhead during an outage.

A container must also fit the task. A sealed bottle for a go-bag, a jug for a household dispenser, and water for a pet may need different arrangements. Do not make everyone depend on a single heavy container that only one person can move.

Clean, fill, and label without confusion

Follow the CDC container-cleaning procedure in the reference below and the container’s instructions before filling a reusable vessel. Use a clean, suitable water source and close the lid tightly. Keep container sanitation separate from drinking-water disinfection: they have different purposes and instructions.

Use durable labels that remain readable in dim light. A simple record might say: “Drinking water; filled 3 October; 2 gallons; replace by 3 April.” Keep the source and any container instructions in the household notebook. If a different container holds water for an allowed non-drinking use, make the distinction unmistakable.

Reference: CDC · Emergency water supply

Store for access as well as capacity

Choose a cool storage place away from direct sunlight, fuel, pesticides, and other chemicals. CDC suggests 50–70°F for stored home-filled water. If your only available space becomes very hot, improve the location rather than assuming any cupboard is suitable. Follow the storage directions and date information for bottled water.

  • Spread manageable containers across appropriate locations so one blocked cupboard or a single leak does not remove all access.
  • Keep frequently used drinking water where household members can reach it without climbing, moving furniture, or relying on power.
  • Use sound support and avoid creating a falling or trip hazard. Consider the combined weight before loading shelves.
  • Keep a small ready-to-carry portion with evacuation essentials; the whole household reserve is not automatically portable.

In an apartment or trailer, sketch the actual spaces you have and label what will go in each. Record both stored gallons and reachable gallons for a person who cannot lift or use stairs. Accessibility is part of the reserve, not an optional refinement after the quantity target is met.

Reference: CDC · Emergency water supply

Make rotation a simple routine

CDC recommends replacing home-filled stored water every six months. Observe the dates on commercially bottled water. Use a calendar reminder and a written shelf label so the routine does not depend on the founder of the household plan remembering everything.

  1. Check containers for damage, leakage, readable labels, and a secure lid.
  2. Use the oldest appropriate supply first in normal household use when suitable, then replace it.
  3. For a refill, repeat the proper cleaning and sanitation process; do not keep topping up an uncertain old supply.
  4. Update the fill date and the inventory with the real volume.
  5. Check whether household numbers, care needs, pet needs, or storage conditions have changed.

Keep the system easy enough that another person can maintain it. A paper inventory needs only container, location, amount, fill or purchase date, and review date. If a container’s history is unclear or it may be contaminated, do not count it as confirmed drinking water.

Reference: CDC · Emergency water supply

Plan meals and dispensing around the reserve

Water use includes what meals require. Write a sample day of familiar food and note every step needing water: preparation, drinking, cleanup, and care. An emergency pantry full of foods that need long boiling can be a poor match for limited water and fuel. Include options that need little or no preparation water.

  • Keep a clean dispensing method with the container. Avoid repeatedly dipping hands or a used cup into the supply.
  • Agree which containers are for everyday use during the disruption and which remain closed as reserve.
  • Allocate suitable water for pets and any provider-directed care requirement.
  • Keep drinking-quality water available for brushing teeth and food preparation during a water advisory, as directed by that notice.

Do a tabletop exercise: list tomorrow’s meals, estimate the water used, then compare it with a day of actual routine use. The exercise reveals omitted tasks without deliberately depriving anyone of water. Use the result to improve storage and reduce unnecessary water-heavy tasks.

Storage is only one layer of the water plan

A reserve buys time; it does not establish an unlimited source. Identify how you would obtain suitable water if the disruption exceeds your stock: a confirmed retailer, distribution point, trusted person, or other verified supply. Record access, carrying capacity, and the possibility that the location is closed.

Do not count swimming-pool water, floodwater, an unknown stream, or chemically contaminated water as drinking supply. Boiling and ordinary filters do not solve every contamination problem. If tap service fails or an advisory is issued, use the water-outage guide to determine the allowed uses and the next safe source.

PLAN AROUND WHAT YOU HAVE

If help is unavailable.

Calculate the household quantity on paper and store smaller containers if lifting a large container is difficult. Keep one portable supply ready. Divide storage between safe places so damage to one container does not lose all the household water.

People & practical needs.

Choose container sizes and dispensing positions that the least mobile household member can use. Include pets, guests, hot-weather needs, pregnancy, illness, and provider-directed care needs in the quantity plan. Ask another capable person to locate and explain the reserve. Keep drinking and non-drinking containers clearly distinct.

Keep essentials within reach

Suitable drinking-water containers, durable labels, a marker, the written quantity and location inventory, and a manageable portable supply. Keep the container-cleaning instructions and rotation dates where another household member can find them.

Next steps.

Learn the local advisory system. Plan how to transport water if distribution sites open.

The stored water cannot be reached safely?

Treat it as unavailable in your plan and change the storage arrangement.

A notice warns of chemicals?

Do not assume boiling or an ordinary filter makes the supply usable; open the matching advisory guide.

Common questions.

What matters before collecting supplies?

Store at least one gallon of water per person per day for three days; aim for two weeks if practical. Allow more for heat, illness, pregnancy and pets. Use commercially bottled water or clean food-grade containers, and keep it cool and away from chemicals. Collect items only if doing so does not delay this action.

What should another household member do?

Use a second person to handle communication or dependents while the first carries out the immediate action. For emergency water storage, keep the specific hazard in view: Do not reuse containers that held toxic substances, even after washing.

What should be written down?

Record when the situation changed, what action was taken and what remains uncertain. For this situation, start with: Rotate supplies. Follow bottled-water dates and replace self-filled stored water every six months. Keep personal notes private; they are not submitted to Osprey Zero.

Watch for.

Do not reuse containers that held toxic substances, even after washing.
