Start with the household you actually have

A useful plan describes who does what when normal arrangements stop working. Begin with the people, pets, places, and services your household relies on. Include anyone who stays part time, someone away at school or work, and a person who may need help communicating or moving. A plan for four fit adults will not work unchanged for a family with a toddler and an adult who cannot use stairs.

Separate immediate escape from planned relocation

Choose a nearby meeting point for escaping a home fire and a separate destination for being unable to return to the neighborhood. These serve different purposes. The fire meeting point is where people can be counted after leaving; it is not necessarily somewhere to sleep. A relocation destination needs a safe route, acceptance, and suitable care and access.

Do not send someone back into a dangerous building to retrieve a bag, pet, or missing person. Tell responders what is known. For a developing regional emergency, preparations can happen before departure becomes urgent: transport, medicines, pets, destination, and an alternate. Keep that longer checklist distinct from immediate escape.

Reference: American Red Cross · Make a plan · USFA · Home fire escape plans

Write the contact and reunion rules

Choose an out-of-area contact who agrees to the role. Give everyone the same contact information on paper as well as in their devices. Decide what to report: who is with you, your condition, where you are, and when you expect to make contact again. A missed message should trigger an agreed, realistic response rather than an improvised search into danger.

  • Record school or daycare pickup rules, authorized collectors, and where children may be taken if the normal site closes.
  • Agree what a person should do if unable to reach the primary meeting point. The alternative must also be safe for the actual incident.
  • Include helpers who can support access or care needs, with permission to share only the information necessary for the task.
  • Keep address and destination information readable without an internet connection. Protect sensitive records; do not post a household plan publicly.

Example: two adults are at work when a neighborhood is evacuated. The plan should explain who can collect the child, where the child will go, and how the other adult learns the new destination. Both adults independently driving to the school can waste time and leave the next decision unclear.

Reference: American Red Cross · Make a plan

Assign tasks and backups without making one person indispensable

Distribute tasks by ability and location. One adult can manage a dependent person while another checks transport and communicates. Someone living alone can still assign an outside check-in contact and keep the essentials organized for self-use. Avoid a plan that requires the only person away at work to come home before any action can begin.

Give tasks clear end points. “Handle the pets” is less useful than “put each pet in its labelled carrier and bring its medicine.” Keep a paper location list for daily-use items that cannot live in a bag. Do not give children responsibility for fuel, electrical work, or decisions about whether a building is safe.

Set decision triggers before an emergency

A trigger states the condition that changes the plan and the action that follows. Use conditions you can recognize: an applicable evacuation order, an unsafe building, an essential device losing backup, or the inability to maintain safe water and sanitation. A vague promise to “leave if things get bad” gives a tired household too much to debate.

  • List the local hazards that could require leaving and those that may require sheltering. The correct response depends on the hazard.
  • For care and equipment, use provider and manufacturer limits. Do not invent a general battery percentage that applies to every person.
  • Choose a time to review stable conditions, but act immediately if an urgent danger develops.
  • Record what information would change a decision, where it comes from, and what you can do if that channel fails.

For an editorial example, a household may decide to relocate while transport remains available if it cannot sustain a particular care need at home. That threshold belongs to the individual care plan. A restoration estimate or social-media rumor should not silently replace it.

Test the plan with a realistic tabletop exercise

Gather the people who use the plan and choose one scenario: power fails while one adult is away and phones are unreliable. Ask each person to explain the next action, not simply agree that the plan looks sensible. Keep essential equipment operating; this exercise is discussion and safe practice, not a deliberate loss of medical support.

  1. Locate the contact card, light, medicines grab list, keys, and relevant bag without turning the exercise into a race.
  2. Describe the first check-in and the response if it cannot be delivered.
  3. Check the destination and route assumptions, including pets and access.
  4. Introduce one change: the driver is unavailable, a road is closed, or the destination cannot accept everyone.
  5. Write down the first three problems and fix the most consequential one before adding more equipment.

Review after moving, a new medical need, a change of school, a new pet, or a different caregiver. Put the review date on the plan and retire confusing old copies. Success means another household member can explain and carry out the plan, not that the folder is impressive.

PLAN AROUND WHAT YOU HAVE

If help is unavailable.

Give each household member a written copy with two meeting places, two contacts and essential medical details. Practise the plan with phones off. Include a destination you can actually reach without an official shelter or rescue transport.

People & practical needs.

Write the plan in formats everyone can use: plain text, large print, translated wording or another needed communication format. Ask each person which assistance they want. Store private identity and health details securely rather than in public shared links.

Keep essentials within reach

Keep the current contact card, role sheet, meeting points, destination notes, care instructions and review date accessible. Keep sensitive household information private. A short usable paper copy is more useful than a file nobody can open.

Next steps.

Keep copies offline and in go bags. Share relevant details with caregivers and trusted contacts.

Common questions.

How long should the written plan be?

Keep the first-action, contact, role and destination information concise. Put equipment manuals and more detailed care information where they can be found separately. Test whether another person can use the plan without the organizer explaining it.

What if a helper is unavailable?

Name another realistic option and mark any unconfirmed arrangement. Review the person’s essential needs directly; do not make the entire plan depend on a single neighbor or a reply to one message.

Watch for.

A plan that depends on one vehicle or one working phone network needs alternatives.
