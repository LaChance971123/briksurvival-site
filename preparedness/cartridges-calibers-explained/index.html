Quick checklist.

  1. 01

    Read the complete designation

    Match the firearm’s chamber marking and manual to the box, including suffixes such as LR, WMR, Luger, NATO and Magnum.

  2. 02

    Separate cartridge from load

    A cartridge identifies the ammunition family. Bullet weight, construction and manufacturer product code identify the particular load within it.

  3. 03

    Compare the actual platform

    Recoil, weight and ammunition availability are properties of the firearm-and-ammunition combination, not caliber alone.

  4. 04

    Check sporting rules

    Hunting rules specify species, seasons, permitted methods and sometimes projectile materials. An emergency does not automatically suspend them.

Caliber, cartridge and bullet: three different things

Caliber often refers to an approximate bore or bullet diameter. Cartridge is the complete ammunition designation: it describes a particular family with its own case and chamber requirements. Bullet is the projectile, not the entire round. A .22 LR cartridge and a .223 Remington cartridge have roughly similar diameter projectiles, yet belong to very different systems. Shared digits do not establish compatibility.

Rimfire ammunition places priming compound in the case rim; centerfire ammunition uses a primer in the center of the base. Both include a case and propellant. A grain is a unit of mass, not a type of powder: bullet weight printed as “124 gr” is not the propellant charge. Box velocity is a result from a stated test setup and may differ in another firearm.

.22 LR and .22 WMR: inexpensive practice and small game

Two rimfire families, not interchangeable versions
CartridgeCommon civilian usesPractical tradeoffs
.22 Long Rifle (.22 LR)Target practice, introductory sporting instruction and small-game hunting where permitted.Usually modest recoil, compact ammunition and a broad selection of target and hunting loads.
.22 Winchester Magnum Rimfire (.22 WMR / .22 Magnum)Small-game and varmint hunting, plus recreational shooting.A different, larger case; generally more report and higher ammunition cost than .22 LR.

For a household that already owns a lawful sporting firearm, .22 LR can make regular range practice relatively affordable. The cartridge is used in rifles and handguns; the action and specific ammunition still affect reliability and the experience. .22 WMR is a distinct chambering, not a “magnum setting” on a .22 LR firearm. Some revolvers have manufacturer-supplied interchangeable cylinders, but that is a model-specific arrangement rather than permission to mix ammunition.

Small-game hunting is a skill and a regulated activity, not an automatic food plan. Species identification, access to permitted land, seasons, humane harvest and food handling all matter. In Florida, for example, rimfire rifles are not a legal method of taking deer. Lightweight ammunition does not remove those limits or make large-game use appropriate.

Handgun cartridges: names, platforms and real tradeoffs

Common centerfire handgun cartridges
CartridgeTypical sporting platformsWhat to understand
9mm Luger / 9×19mmSemiautomatic pistols and some sporting carbines.Widely encountered; its common name does not include every cartridge beginning with “9mm.”
.380 Auto / .380 ACP / 9mm Browning ShortOften compact semiautomatic pistols.Distinct from 9mm Luger. A lighter firearm can feel sharper or be harder to handle than the smaller cartridge suggests.
.38 SpecialCommonly revolvers; also some sporting rifles.A rimmed cartridge. It is not .38 Super or .380 Auto.
.45 Auto / .45 ACPSemiautomatic pistols and some sporting carbines.Larger cartridge and typically heavier projectiles than common 9mm loads; not .45 Colt.

A comfortable range firearm depends on grip fit, overall mass, controls and the particular load. A compact pistol is easy to store but may have a less forgiving grip and sight picture than a larger sporting model. It is therefore misleading to promise that a cartridge is “easy for everyone” or rank the whole experience by bullet diameter alone.

Suffixes matter. +P denotes a higher-pressure designation for certain standardized cartridges; it is not an upgrade for any firearm bearing the base name. +P+ is not a single universal SAAMI pressure standard. The firearm manufacturer must authorize the exact ammunition category. Keep ordinary and special-pressure products distinctly labelled rather than combining loose rounds because the headstamps look similar.

.223, 5.56 and the three very different “7.62” names

Centerfire rifle families
DesignationCommon sporting contextImportant distinction
.223 RemingtonTarget, varmint and other hunting uses where permitted.Closely related to 5.56×45 NATO, but chamber specifications and ammunition requirements are not identical.
5.56×45 NATOCommon in rifles also used for organized recreational shooting.Do not assume a .223-marked chamber accepts 5.56 ammunition.
.308 WinchesterTarget shooting and game hunting with a suitable load and lawful method.Closely related to 7.62×51 NATO; verify the exact firearm’s authorization.
7.62×51 NATOSome rifles used in sporting and target activities.The “51” distinguishes this family from 7.62×39.
7.62×39Sporting rifles used for recreational shooting and permitted hunting.A different case and chamber from .308 Winchester / 7.62×51.

SAAMI explains that .223 Remington ammunition can generally be used in a manufacturer-confirmed military-specification 5.56 chamber, while 5.56 ammunition should not be assumed safe in a .223 Remington chamber. A .223 Wylde marking refers to a chamber design, not a separate cartridge on the shelf; consult that firearm’s manual. The useful lesson is specific compatibility, not an improvised fit test.

A number such as “7.62” gives too little information to choose ammunition. Case length, shape, chamber standards and intended loading differ. Likewise, “.308 and NATO are the same” is too broad for every firearm, including older examples. Rifle cartridges also tend to add ammunition bulk and report compared with rimfire. Hunting suitability depends on the species, projectile, firearm and law; there is no universal caliber that solves every outdoor task.

12 and 20 gauge: read the whole shotshell label

Gauge is a historical bore-size system, not a caliber in millimeters. Within this system 12 gauge is a larger bore than 20 gauge; .410 bore is an exception named by bore diameter. A shotgun may use shot, a group of pellets, or a slug, a single projectile. Those are different load types within the shotgun family, not different names for the firearm.

What a shotshell box tells you
LabelMeaning
GaugeThe shell family must match the firearm. Do not mix 12 and 20 gauge in one container.
Shell lengthThe designated length refers to the fired/opened shell, not simply the unfired length in your hand. Match chamber authorization.
Shot sizeFor common numbered birdshot, a higher number generally means smaller pellets. Buckshot uses another naming scale.
PayloadThe quantity of shot, often in ounces; separate from pellet diameter.
MaterialLead, steel and other materials have different properties; hunting and firearm restrictions can apply.

Clay sports and bird hunting often use shotshells selected for the rules of that activity. Bigger gauge, heavier payload or “magnum” branding does not automatically mean better results. Firearm fit, total weight and the particular load affect recoil. Check both firearm and ammunition instructions; a shell slipping into an opening does not prove it belongs there.

Build a comparison that answers your actual question

For a sporting purchase, write down the activity first: affordable paper-target practice, a particular licensed hunting activity or an established club discipline. Compare the cost of routine ammunition, range fees, instruction, secure storage and maintenance as well as the firearm. Availability in your area matters more than an attractive specification from a product you cannot regularly obtain.

  • Record the full chamber designation, exact model and manufacturer-approved ammunition categories.
  • Compare prices per round within the same cartridge and intended sporting load type.
  • Keep product codes and lot numbers when you buy; they make recall checks possible.
  • Reject simple “bigger is better” claims. Cartridge names alone do not describe reliability, accuracy or suitability.
  • Use the cartridge guide alongside the ammunition-storage guide; identification and condition are separate checks.

For preparedness, preserve the sporting equipment you already understand before adding unfamiliar systems. A labeled, modest inventory that remains secure, dry and identifiable is easier to manage than assorted loose ammunition. Skills, lawful access and realistic expectations give the equipment a purpose; the cartridge label alone does not provide one.

Read an ammunition label as separate fields

Treat the package as several descriptions rather than one caliber number. The recognized cartridge designation identifies the cartridge family. The bullet or shot description, stated projectile weight, package count and product/lot identification describe that particular product. These fields answer different questions. A weight printed in grains describes mass; it is not a powder-loading instruction.

Illustrative label-reading worksheet
Example fieldWhat it tells youWhat remains to check
9mm LugerA complete cartridge designation, rather than the broad phrase “9mm.”The exact firearm manual and any additional product restrictions.

These are invented worksheet entries, not a recommendation for a particular firearm. Copy a real package’s full wording before comparing products. Product names can sound nearly identical while identifying different offerings. Keep the original label and lot reference with stored ammunition; a handwritten caliber abbreviation drops information you may need for a recall or manufacturer question.

Compare cost and carried mass without a performance ranking

Separate compatibility from preference. First confirm that a product is appropriate for the exact firearm and permitted for the sporting activity and location. Then compare the things that affect ownership: availability, packaging, recurring purchase cost, storage space and actual transport mass. A cartridge family does not have one fixed price or one universal legal sporting use.

For a sporting comparison, write the relevant discipline or species, land-manager rules, ammunition restrictions, likely availability near the destination and your qualified instructor’s or outfitter’s requirements. Do not turn “larger,” “magnum” or a numerical energy listing into a universal recommendation. For hunting, verify current rules for that jurisdiction and species; the linked Florida example applies to Florida and cannot establish rules elsewhere.

When identification is uncertain.

Set unidentified or mixed ammunition aside in secured, clearly marked packaging. Do not try it in a firearm to discover whether it fits. A photograph of markings and the original box can help the manufacturer identify the product.

Keep ammunition families distinct.

Use full names on storage labels, including gauge and shell length where relevant. Anyone authorized to manage the inventory should understand that similar numbers and similar appearance do not establish compatibility.

Information to keep with the inventory

Manufacturer, full cartridge name, product code, lot number, quantity, purchase date and the exact firearm manual.

Make your cartridge reference.

List the chambering of each sporting firearm you own and link the corresponding manufacturer manual. Use that list for purchases rather than memory.

Essential handling note.

Ammunition remains hazardous regardless of its size. Keep it secured and correctly identified; never force a cartridge into a firearm or use damaged, recalled or disaster-exposed ammunition without the manufacturer’s clearance.
