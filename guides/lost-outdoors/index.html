Lost outdoors

Do now ↓

QUICK ANSWER · START HERE

Stop moving, stay together and assess your last known position. If there is no immediate threat, stay put, protect yourself from weather and signal for help. Share your location if communication works; do not wander in search of a shortcut.

Do now.

  1. 01

    Stop and assess

    Check injury, weather, daylight and remaining supplies. Mark your location and the last reliable landmark on an offline map if available.

  2. 02

    Choose a safe waiting place

    Move only enough to escape immediate hazards such as floodwater, falling trees or exposed severe weather. Keep the group together and tell contacts if your location changes.

  3. 03

    Protect from exposure

    Use available layers and shelter to reduce wet, wind, cold or sun exposure. Avoid hazardous terrain while trying to improve a shelter.

  4. 04

    Signal and conserve

    Use a whistle, bright material or light when appropriate. Call or send a location message if possible; conserve phone power between attempts.

Avoid making the search area larger

When you realize the route or position is uncertain, stop unnecessary movement and keep the group together. NPS recommends staying put when lost unless there is an immediate threat to life. Establish the safest workable waiting position and the best location information available.

Keep the last confirmed fact distinct from a guess. Write the last reliable landmark, approximate time and what happened afterward. Do not turn “we think that was the junction” into a certain starting point for a new journey. Use the map, signs and available device information without discarding a clear mismatch on the ground.

Avoid unplanned downhill travel, river following or a guessed shortcut to a road. The desire to take action is understandable, but action should have a clear protective purpose rather than merely create distance from the known itinerary.

Make a precise help message

Give current coordinates with their format if available, or the last confirmed landmark and time. State whether the position is known or estimated, the number of people, urgent needs, immediate hazards and the intended action. Keep a reply method available.

For example, use the structure “Location [known fact and uncertainty]; people [number and relevant needs]; event [what changed]; hazards [current conditions]; intention [remain or necessary protective move]; contact [available channel].” Fill it with real facts. Do not send invented practice coordinates as an emergency message.

Read back numbers when speaking to a recipient. A map pin may help only if it can be transmitted and opened. If a message cannot be delivered, preserve it for a later suitable attempt and use the equipment’s instructions for power management. Record the attempt and any response so the group does not confuse a saved draft with contact achieved.

Choose a waiting place for protection and discovery

The waiting place should support the people while keeping the location usable for contact or search. Assess nearby water changes, overhead hazards, unstable ground and exposure. Do not climb or enter difficult terrain simply for a more visible position or better phone display.

The choices are conditional. “Stay put” does not require remaining in rising water or under a damaged branch. Conversely, discomfort alone should not become a speculative journey with no known destination. If an actual responder provides appropriate instructions, clarify anything unclear and communicate the group’s real ability and needs.

Reference: NPS · Outdoor emergency plan

Make the available weather system usable

Find the protected layers and suitable shelter equipment, then organize the occupied area around the actual people. Use ground insulation and dry sleeping items where appropriate. Keep lights, care equipment, water and the communication device accessible instead of completing a complicated camp before meeting immediate needs.

Use the equipment’s tested configuration and instructions. A roof alone is not a complete cold-weather system, and an improvised fire is not a guaranteed source of warmth. Never bring a fuel-burning appliance into a tent or tarp enclosure. If the conditions exceed the equipment, explain that in the help request and continue achievable protection without hazardous improvisation.

Assign simple tasks by ability. One adult can arrange the suitable layers while another records location and contact attempts. Keep assistance with a person who needs it. Do not make everyone unpack every bag or separate the group to collect speculative materials.

Manage water, food and energy during the delay

Inventory what is actually available and keep necessary drinking water and familiar food accessible. Separate usable supplies from empty containers or products requiring equipment you do not have. Include individual care requirements and the time needed for any appropriate water treatment.

Reduce avoidable exertion such as repeated uncertain walks or prolonged shouting. Do not withhold essential hydration to follow an invented endurance rule. If a natural source is being considered, the source and treatment hazard matter; clear water is not automatically suitable. Use the selected product’s instructions and the known plan rather than improvising a dose.

Keep a short record of supplies used and changing needs without turning the wait into a contest. Share appropriate food and equipment according to actual requirements, not the assumption that every person has identical needs. Worsening symptoms or an urgent care gap should be communicated as facts through the available appropriate channel.

Reference: NPS · Water treatment

Coordinate the people and the signals

Give each person a clear place in the waiting arrangement and count the group after any necessary move. Use an appropriate whistle, personal light or visible material from a suitable position. Do not assume a signal has been noticed simply because it was used. Keep local signals separate from an electronic distress alert and follow the actual device instructions.

If a child is lost, the prior family plan should emphasize stopping and staying findable rather than hiding because they fear blame. Reassure the person and use age-appropriate instructions. For sensory or communication needs, use the familiar accessible method and a calm helper.

If another person is missing, keep the remaining group coordinated and report the known last location, time, identifying information and relevant needs through the appropriate plan. An unplanned off-trail search can create additional lost people. Do not send a lone unprepared participant on a speculative search for help or the missing person.

Reference: NPS · Outdoor emergency plan

Use accountability and close the incident accurately

The trusted trip contact should already have the intended route and overdue action. That arrangement remains valuable when reception fails. If contact works, explain any change in location or intention and state the next update when feasible. Keep the device available for follow-up rather than packing it away after one attempt.

When the situation is resolved, confirm return or transfer of care with the contact and any appropriate responder. Do not leave an active overdue plan running. Afterward, record the factual sequence while it is fresh: last confirmed position, uncertainty, weather, equipment access, messages and decisions.

  1. Confirm everyone is accounted for and any continuing care need is handled.
  2. Close the trip/overdue arrangement with the trusted contact.
  3. Replenish used supplies and inspect the equipment.
  4. Identify the first point where the plan became uncertain.
  5. Make one practical route, equipment or communication correction.
  6. Use supported instruction before taking on a more demanding trip.

PLAN AROUND WHAT YOU HAVE

If help is unavailable.

Use the supplies and shelter you already have; do not assume a rescue is underway until communication confirms it. Leave a clear note of time, direction and destination only if immediate danger forces a move. Avoid dividing the party or attempting unverified routes in darkness.

People & practical needs.

Keep children and less mobile companions beside an adult. Check everyone for cold, heat and exhaustion and inventory each person’s supplies. Before a trip, leave a route, return time and overdue plan with a trusted contact.

Keep essentials within reach

Accessible weather protection and ground insulation, drinking water and food, individual light and signal, appropriate map, communication device and power, necessary care and the trip-contact information.

Next steps.

Give rescuers coordinates, last known location, group size, injuries and equipment. When a location is shared, stay there unless a direct threat forces a move.

Immediate hazard where you stopped?

Reach a nearby safer position with the group, record it and update contact if possible.

Suitable site but position uncertain?

Remain findable, organize protection and send the best factual location information.

No phone service?

Use the prepared equipment and trip-contact plan; do not roam solely for reception.

Another person is missing?

Keep the remaining group coordinated and use the appropriate reporting plan; avoid an unplanned off-trail search.

Common questions.

Should I follow a stream downhill?

No universal rule makes that a suitable route. Watercourses can introduce cliffs, flooding or difficult access. Use the known facts and the lost-person plan rather than a shortcut guess.

Can staying put still require moving?

Yes, if the current site has an immediate threat. Make the protective purpose clear, keep the group together and record/update the new position.

Does sending a message mean rescue is on the way?

No. Distinguish attempted, delivered and acknowledged messages, follow received instructions and continue practical protection.

Watch for.

A phone compass or GPS reading is not a safe route by itself. Do not climb hazardous ridges for a signal or enter moving water.
