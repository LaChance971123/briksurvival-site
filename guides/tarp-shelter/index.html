Emergency tarp shelter and dry sleeping systems

Build your plan ↓

QUICK ANSWER · START HERE

A shelter needs a safe site, wind and rain protection, and insulation from the ground. Test your tarp and sleeping equipment before a trip; a tarp alone does not make cold or severe weather safe.

Build your plan.

  1. 01

    Choose the site first

    Avoid drainage channels, rising water, unstable slopes, cliff edges and dead or damaged overhead branches. Move to a safer location before arranging equipment.

  2. 02

    Protect from ground moisture

    Use a ground barrier and insulation suited to conditions. Keep your sleep area above local runoff; do not dig unauthorized drainage trenches.

  3. 03

    Pitch and check

    Use the shelter’s instructions and existing suitable anchors. Keep fabric tensioned so rain does not pool. Check that stakes and cord do not create trip hazards.

  4. 04

    Keep warmth and airflow

    Protect dry layers and your sleeping insulation. Maintain ventilation appropriate to the shelter. Never use fuel-burning heaters or cooking equipment inside an improvised shelter.

A complete shelter system

Think in separate layers: overhead weather barrier, ground protection, ground insulation, sleeping insulation and dry clothing. Each has a different job. A tarp can reduce wind or rain exposure in an appropriate configuration; it does not provide the ground insulation of a pad or the sleeping insulation of suitable bedding.

Test the complete arrangement at home or a permitted supported site before a trip. Include the bags, shoes, personal light and space needed to assist another person. A shelter that technically fits two sleeping bodies may not provide the usable space the household needs for care or an orderly exit.

Know when to leave

A tarp is an equipment choice with limits, not a substitute for every building or tent. Set the early-return or alternative-shelter decision before the trip. Check weather, daylight, the group’s condition and the manufacturer’s stated limits for the complete equipment.

An apparently sheltered hollow can collect water; overhead damaged branches and exposed edges introduce other hazards. Do not settle on an unsuitable site merely because it is easier to pitch there. If the conditions exceed the system or the group’s ability, change the plan while the known return remains practical.

These are alternatives. A tarp does not make severe wind, flooding or a thunderstorm safe. If immediate danger develops, protect the people first and seek appropriate help; do not stay to finish a tidy pitch or retrieve nonessential equipment.

Reference: NPS · Hike smart

Select the site before unpacking the system

Review the occupied area and surrounding hazards in daylight when possible. Identify drainage, water changes, overhead condition, slope, access and the route back to a known place. Choose permitted durable ground and follow the site’s current rules. A visually flat patch is not the entire assessment.

Think about the whole group: a dependent person may need room for assistance, and a mobility aid may require a suitable approach. Plan an entry that can be found in darkness and keep the path clear of loose equipment. Do not dig trenches, cut branches or create new structural anchors as a default way to force the site to fit the equipment.

Use a tested configuration and inspect it

Follow the selected shelter’s instructions and compatible supplied or manufacturer-approved supports. Practice the actual configuration before departure, including cord access and the tools needed for it. Do not assume every tarp, attachment point or support can accept the same arrangement.

During ordinary setup, keep the occupied area and exits clear, arrange the ground and sleep layers, then check the overhead coverage and ventilation appropriate to the product. Look for sagging areas where water may collect and cord or stake positions that create a trip problem. Use the maker’s instructions for any adjustment; this guide does not provide structural loads or a universal knot/anchor recipe.

Check the pitch again after a meaningful weather change and before sleeping. Account for everyone’s access and the location of lights, shoes and care equipment. If the setup cannot remain usable without continual improvised fixes, reassess the configuration or site rather than treating persistence as proof of adequacy.

Reference: NPS · Ten essentials

Protect the dry sleeping area

Organize the shelter so the dry system remains protected during entry and packing. Keep wet outer equipment separate from bedding where practical, and leave enough room to handle the care tasks the group actually needs. Mark which bag contains the protected sleeping layers so another person can find them.

Use the pad and bedding according to their instructions. Compare the manufacturer’s conditions and rating method rather than assuming one printed temperature has the same meaning across every product. Include the person’s fit and care needs in the decision. If a system has not been tested in ordinary supported use, do not discover its limits on an isolated severe-weather trip.

A foil emergency blanket can be one contingency item, but it is not equivalent to a complete sleeping system. Do not pack it as the sole solution to an expected cold overnight stay. Keep the return or suitable-shelter plan available instead of depending on an improvised heat source.

Reference: NPS · Ten essentials

Keep cooking and combustion outside the shelter

Plan food that can be eaten as carried when the trip must work without fire. Fuel-burning appliances can generate carbon monoxide; CDC warns against indoor use of portable fuel-burning appliances. Never use a stove, charcoal, lantern or improvised heater inside a tent or tarp enclosure to replace missing insulation.

For any permitted outdoor cooking, use the exact appliance’s siting and operation instructions and current local restrictions. An open entrance or a cracked edge does not certify a safe combustion arrangement. Keep hot equipment and incompatible chemicals away from sleeping and care materials.

The simplest shelter meal is one that introduces no setup problem: suitable ready-to-eat food, a workable container and the needed utensils. Check the package while planning and keep water needs visible. If warm food is important to the trip, resolve a suitable permitted cooking system separately; do not conceal it inside the shelter plan.

Reference: CDC · Carbon monoxide

Rehearse the full system and leave the site usable

Practice at a permitted supported location with an easy stop and all real help available. Test entry, exit, dry-layer access, personal lighting and how equipment is packed again. A photograph of an empty pitch cannot show whether the people can use it.

  1. Inventory the complete shelter and sleep layers.
  2. Choose a suitable permitted practice site and read the instructions.
  3. Use the intended compatible configuration.
  4. Place the actual bedding, bags and care equipment.
  5. Check access and lighting without creating a real cold or wet exposure test.
  6. Pack and inspect the system, carry out waste and record one correction.

After ordinary use, follow the manufacturer’s cleaning and drying instructions before storage. Account for stakes, cords and small fittings. Leave the site without trenches, cut vegetation or scraps. Correct missing parts at home so the next trip does not begin with an untested substitute.

PLAN AROUND WHAT YOU HAVE

If help is unavailable.

Use the safest reachable shelter and keep the group together. Reduce wind exposure and ground heat loss with available equipment; do not undertake a risky climb or river crossing to find a prettier campsite.

People & practical needs.

Fit shelter and sleeping systems to actual body sizes, mobility needs and weather. A person who cannot help pitch the tarp still needs a dry, insulated place and an accessible exit.

Keep essentials within reach

A complete weather-appropriate shelter/sleep system: tarp or selected shelter, supplied compatible supports and cords, ground barrier, insulating pad, sleeping insulation, protected dry layers and personal lights. Keep the instructions with the kit.

Next steps.

Pitch your actual shelter before dark, simulate rain with a hose at home, and check pooling, runoff and exit access.

The site has a hazard?

Choose a nearby suitable position before arranging shelter.

The complete system is inadequate for forecast conditions?

Use a suitable alternative or return early; a roof alone is not sufficient.

The pitch needs unfamiliar improvised supports?

Use the tested manufacturer-supported configuration or change the plan.

Common questions.

Does a tarp keep a person warm by itself?

It can provide a weather barrier, but ground insulation, sleeping insulation, clothing and conditions determine whether the complete system is suitable.

Can I use a stove under the shelter to compensate?

Do not use fuel-burning cooking or heating equipment inside a tent or improvised shelter. It creates carbon-monoxide and fire risks rather than fixing inadequate insulation.

Watch for.

Improvised anchors are not climbing protection. Do not cut structural branches, sleep beneath unstable trees or rely on a campfire for overnight warmth.
