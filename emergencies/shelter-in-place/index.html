Shelter-in-place

QUICK ANSWER · START HERE

Get inside if the outdoor hazard makes travel more dangerous, and choose the room for that hazard: a lowest-floor sturdy interior area for a tornado, protective interior mass for fallout, or few outside openings for an airborne chemical release. Close openings and control ventilation only as the hazard requires. Do not remain in a burning, flooding or collapsing building, and do not wait for a message when immediate danger is visible.

The phrase does not identify the correct room

Shelter-in-place means using a protective location rather than travelling through a hazard. It does not describe one universal room or ventilation setting. Identify the actual threat, affected area, building conditions, and current instructions before applying a familiar checklist. A tornado room and a chemical-release room can require different choices.

Choose and prepare locations before the event

Identify suitable spaces for the hazards that actually affect your area. Record how to reach each, how everyone can move there, which supplies are accessible, and what building systems may matter. Someone unable to use stairs may need an advance alternative rather than a last-minute promise that a high or low floor can be reached.

Do a safe walk to each location and check the actual doors, furniture, and walking space. Keep the path usable without relying on powered lighting. Store a light and relevant information where they can be reached. Avoid turning the room into storage so dense that people, pets, and care equipment cannot fit.

For a chemical instruction, control outside air as directed

CDC’s general chemical-emergency guidance calls for getting indoors, closing windows and doors, turning off fans, air conditioning and the furnace, and moving to the chosen room. It describes temporarily sealing openings with appropriate supplies. Follow the actual incident instruction and equipment limits; this is not a general ventilation rule for every emergency.

Prepare supplies and identify controls in advance if this hazard is relevant. Mark which controls belong to your dwelling and which are shared building systems. Ask management about shared ventilation before a crisis; do not dismantle unfamiliar equipment, block safety systems, or assume a room is airtight because its door closes.

Never add combustion to a sealed shelter

Do not use a generator, grill, outdoor stove, or improvised fuel-burning heater in the room. Sealing does not protect people from a hazard created inside the shelter. Keep an immediate escape option and seek urgent help for symptoms or another internal danger.

Plastic and tape provide a temporary protective measure when the specific instruction calls for it; they are not proof of indefinite safe air or guaranteed protection. Keep following updates, and use the incident’s directions for ending the shelter period. Do not invent a universal number of hours for a sealed room.

Reference: CDC · Chemical emergency sheltering · American Red Cross · Power outage safety

Bring essentials without delaying protection

Move people and necessary assistance first. Collect medicines, essential equipment, phone power, a radio or other accessible alert source, safe water, and pet necessities only when doing so does not delay the protective action. Avoid repeatedly leaving the shelter to collect conveniences.

  • Count everyone at entry and keep a named adult responsible for each dependent person.
  • Choose a light that people can use safely and keep walking routes clear.
  • Keep a concise contact message ready: location, people present, immediate needs, and next planned check-in.
  • Place food and drinking water apart from waste and personal-care activity.
  • Respect medicine and device instructions; ordinary supplies in the room do not replace an individual care backup.

A shelter plan should include how a person receives instructions if they cannot hear an alarm, read a phone screen, or understand the language used. Arrange accessible communication rather than assuming another resident will translate or assist. A familiar comfort item may help a child if it is already available, but retrieving it must not delay sheltering.

Reference: CDC · Chemical emergency sheltering · American Red Cross · Make a plan

Monitor both the outside threat and the inside conditions

Write down the latest instruction, its source, time, and affected area. Keep a reliable information channel available while conserving unnecessary device use. A social-media message from another neighborhood may not apply to the building or hazard you are facing. Updates can change the safer action.

Conditions to reassess
ChangeDecision to make
Fire, gas, flooding, structural damage, or CO alarm insideLeave the immediate danger using appropriate escape guidance; seek help from a safe place.

No clear all-clearDo not treat silence or reduced smell as proof the hazard has ended.

Avoid two extremes: staying regardless of an unsafe building, and going outside simply because waiting is uncomfortable. The decision needs the actual hazard and the household’s condition. If guidance is unavailable, retain the best known protective arrangement while watching for immediate internal danger.

Reference: CDC · Chemical emergency sheltering

Ending the shelter period and improving the plan

Use the applicable all-clear and recovery instructions. For a chemical incident, follow directions about leaving, ventilation, and possible exposure rather than removing seals and opening every window on a guess. Tornado recovery and fallout recovery have different hazards. A quieter street is not a universal clearance signal.

  1. Account for the household and check urgent care needs.
  2. Confirm which instruction applies to leaving the room or building.
  3. Avoid damaged utility areas, debris, or other remaining hazards.
  4. Replenish the supplies used and restore the room without obstructing exits.
  5. Record one access problem, one information problem, and any equipment misunderstanding.
  6. Update the hazard-specific room plan and explain the changes to everyone who uses it.

A tabletop practice can ask which room the household would use for two different hazards and why. Keep the exercise low risk: do not deliberately seal occupied rooms, disable essential care systems, or create a ventilation problem. Finding an incorrect assumption before an emergency is the useful outcome.

Reference: CDC · Chemical emergency sheltering

PLAN AROUND WHAT YOU HAVE

If help is unavailable.

Choose the room for the hazard, not the phrase: lowest sturdy interior area for tornadoes, protective interior mass for fallout, and a room with few outside openings for an airborne chemical threat. Leave a shelter that is burning, flooding or collapsing rather than following a generic stay-inside rule.

People & practical needs.

Plan a reachable shelter space for each relevant hazard. Assign help for children, pets and anyone with mobility or communication needs; count people at entry. Keep prescribed care and an accessible alert method available. Do not assume the same room or ventilation rule is suitable for every threat.

Keep essentials within reach

People, pets, care essentials, light, phone power, an accessible alert source and safe water. Keep hazard-specific shelter information available. Collect items only if doing so does not delay protection.

Next steps.

Plan safe rooms for the hazards in your area and keep an alert source that works during outages.

Common questions.

Is the basement always the best room?

No. Tornado and fallout guidance often favor low or central protective areas, while CDC chemical-release guidance uses different room criteria. Identify the hazard and follow its specific instructions.

Does silence mean the hazard is over?

No. Loss of messages, reduced noise or absence of an odor does not establish an all-clear. Keep using an appropriate information channel and reassess actual inside conditions.

Watch for.

Do not apply chemical room-sealing instructions to every hazard or remain in a burning building.
