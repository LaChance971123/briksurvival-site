Power outage

QUICK ANSWER · START HERE

Deal first with essential medical power, unsafe heat or cold, and drinking water. Use battery light, conserve phone power and keep fridge doors closed. Assume restoration may take longer than expected. Run generators outdoors at least 20 feet from openings; never heat a home with an oven, grill or indoor generator. Relocate early if you cannot maintain essential care or safe temperature.

Do now.

  1. 01

    Check the situation

    Find the outage map and local emergency alerts. If someone depends on powered medical equipment, use the backup plan and seek urgent help if that equipment cannot run.

  2. 02

    Make the space safe

    Use flashlights rather than candles. Keep refrigerators and freezers closed. Unplug sensitive electronics if power is unstable, and leave one light on to show when service returns.

  3. 03

    Keep air safe

    A generator, camp stove, grill, or vehicle can produce deadly carbon monoxide. Keep fuel-burning equipment outside and well away from openings; use a battery-powered CO alarm if available.

  4. 04

    Check your people

    Contact neighbors who may need help. Conserve phone power, use text when calls are congested, and monitor official updates.

First 15 minutes: make the household safe

Start with people rather than equipment. Find everyone, ask who needs help, and put a battery light where the household can reach it. If someone depends on powered medical equipment, make that person’s backup plan the first decision. A fridge full of food is replaceable; a failing essential device is not.

Confirm whether the outage affects the neighborhood using a safe view outside or your utility’s outage channel. Do not walk toward damaged lines or open an electrical panel to investigate. A single building with burning smells or visible damage needs qualified assessment, not repeated attempts to reset breakers.

Set up one practical household base

Choose an accessible room for information, lights, charging cables, and the household notebook. Keep exits clear and avoid turning the floor into a maze of extension cords. Everyone should know where to find a light without using a phone screen or searching a dark cupboard.

  • Assign a check-in person, a food-and-water person, and an information person when there are enough adults. One person can hold all three roles in a small household.
  • Write down who is home, who is away, the outage start time, and the next contact attempt. Include pets and people staying temporarily.
  • Send one concise message to an outside contact: location, everyone’s condition, immediate needs, and when you will check in again.
  • Keep a normal exit available. Test whether electronic locks, powered garage doors, or building access systems create a problem before you need to leave.

If a neighbor offers help, be specific: a charged phone, a safe ride, a welfare check, or a place for someone with temperature-sensitive needs. Agree how to reconnect if phones fail. Avoid promising regular support that you cannot sustain yourself. This is household coordination, not an assumption that outside services will arrive.

A portable battery is useful only when the device can actually connect to it and its output can handle the load. Read the appliance and battery instructions. Power rating in watts describes what can run at a given moment; energy in watt-hours describes stored capacity. These are different limits. A large energy number does not guarantee enough output for a compressor or other high-starting-load appliance.

Medical equipment needs an individual plan

Do not experiment with power connections, change prescribed settings, or assume a battery’s advertised runtime applies to a medical device. Use provider and manufacturer guidance, and arrange a fallback before remaining backup becomes too small to move safely.

Food, water, and cooking during the first day

Write the outage start time on a note beside the refrigerator and stop opening it to check whether it feels cold. Decide what you can eat from the shelf before fetching cold food. Keep food decisions separate from medicine decisions: the same storage rule does not apply to both.

  • Use the food-safety guide for temperature checks, the closed-door time limits, and discard decisions. Smell or a quick taste cannot establish safety.
  • Choose familiar shelf-stable meals that do not require cooking or a large volume of water. Check allergies, texture needs, and whether the packaging needs an opener.
  • If a private well uses an electric pump, count stored drinking water now. Municipal water may also be disrupted; look for an actual advisory rather than assuming the tap is safe.
  • Use outdoor cooking equipment only where its instructions and local conditions permit. A garage or open window does not convert outdoor equipment into an indoor appliance.

Decide when staying home stops working

The duration of the outage is only one factor. A person in an accessible home with mild weather and working medical backup may have very different options from someone on an upper floor with a failed lift. Choose a decision point based on the need most likely to fail, not on optimism about the restoration estimate.

Confirm that a proposed destination is open and suitable before a non-urgent trip. Ask about accessibility, charging, pets, and any specific care requirement. Take medicines, identification, charging cables, essential equipment, and your contact sheet. A destination written in a plan is not a guaranteed open shelter.

When the electricity returns

  1. Check for damage from a safe position. Flooded or damaged wiring and appliances require professional assessment; do not energize suspect equipment.
  2. Restore ordinary appliances in a controlled sequence according to their instructions, rather than restarting every high-demand device at once.
  3. Make food decisions using the recorded time and temperatures. Power returning does not erase earlier unsafe storage.
  4. Check medically important supplies with the pharmacist or care provider when their required storage conditions were not maintained.
  5. Recharge used batteries, replace consumed supplies, and write down what failed while it is still fresh.

Restoration can be intermittent. Keep lights and your household base available until power is stable. If the original cause affected drinking water or sewage, wait for the relevant service instructions as well. Electrical restoration alone does not certify other systems as safe.

Practice before the next outage

Run a short, low-risk evening exercise with ordinary lights switched off; leave essential medical, refrigeration, and safety systems operating. Ask each person to find a light, locate the contact sheet, identify a no-cook meal, and explain where they would go if the home became unsuitable. Do not simulate hazardous wiring changes or operate combustion equipment for the exercise.

  • Record any item that was missing, inaccessible, uncharged, or unfamiliar.
  • Fix one practical problem at a time: a light by the bed, the correct cable, a paper number, or a confirmed ride.
  • Review after household changes, a new medical need, or new equipment. Let another capable adult explain the plan back in their own words.
Planning for a longer blackout

PLAN AROUND WHAT YOU HAVE

If help is unavailable.

Assume restoration time is uncertain. Reserve battery power for medical equipment, light and short check-ins, keep fridge doors closed and inventory safe water. If essential medical support or safe temperature cannot be maintained, move to a checked safer place early rather than waiting for an estimate.

People & practical needs.

Choose one person to manage medical power and one to handle food, water and short contact check-ins. Keep children away from candles, electrical damage and generator fuel. Check whether a neighbor has the same immediate needs before assuming they can help.

Keep essentials within reach

Battery light or headlamp, working charging cables, charged power bank, paper contacts, essential medicines and care information, safe drinking water, and a no-cook meal. Keep mobility aids and any prescribed backup equipment accessible. Do not delay leaving an immediate hazard to collect supplies.

Next steps.

If the outage continues, plan for safe temperature, water, medication refrigeration, and a place to relocate if the home becomes unsafe. Check current food-safety advice before eating perishables after a prolonged outage.

Essential care cannot continue?

Use the prearranged care or relocation plan immediately. Do not wait for a restoration estimate.

No safe light?

Use an available battery light and keep movement simple; do not add indoor combustion hazards.

Common questions.

Who depends on power?

Name the person whose medicine, mobility, temperature control or breathing support fails first. Ask their care team for a device-specific backup plan before an outage. Record battery runtime and a reachable alternative location; a power bank is not automatically compatible with medical equipment.

What if restoration keeps slipping?

Set a household reassessment time based on the shortest essential battery or temperature limit. Check the destination before moving. Do not wait for a utility estimate to become a guarantee.

Watch for.

Headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, or confusion near fuel-burning equipment can signal carbon monoxide exposure. Get into fresh air and call emergency services. Never enter a flooded area to operate electrical equipment.
